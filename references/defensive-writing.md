# Defensive-writing review

Apply this checklist to every delegated chapter, caption, table note, abstract, introduction and conclusion. A sentence is defensive when it protects the author or narrates the writing/retrieval process, and removing it would not change what the reader learns about the research. Delete or rewrite it. Do not confuse this with a scientifically necessary limitation.

## Remove or relocate

1. **Pre-emptive disclaimers:** broad statements such as “we do not claim to cover all literature,” “the conclusions are not universal,” or “this is not a causal claim” that add no evidence. State the actual scope, result or relation directly. Report a review's search method and scope in its designated methods section when needed for reproducibility.
2. **Access-process narration:** “the full text was unavailable,” “the DOI page returned 403,” “only the abstract was checked,” or “this round did not retrieve the source.” Keep retrieval status and access level in the evidence ledger and audit files. In prose, use only claims supported by the material actually inspected; obtain stronger evidence or omit unsupported detail.
3. **Self-rated evidence:** “abstract-level evidence,” “not independently verified,” “based on searchable PDF snippets,” “the authors report,” or “confirmed from the accessible excerpt” when the phrase merely describes the agent's own checking process. Use a precise source locator in the evidence record and cite the source. Never turn a weakly supported claim into an unqualified assertion: narrow it to the verified fact or remove it.
4. **Defensive negation:** repeated “this review does not treat,” “should not be regarded as,” or “not intended to prove” language. When the boundary matters, state it directly: for example, “Video-counting results are contextual evidence, not core single-image counting results.”
5. **NR placeholders:** do not fill prose or tables with NR, “not checked,” or “not verified” as substitutes for absent information. Omit unknown fields unless the target venue or a meaningful comparison requires an explicit missing-data marker.
6. **Repeated table cautions:** remove cell-level reminders such as “not directly comparable” or “control for backbone differences” when the table already states the relevant protocol facts or the surrounding text explains the condition. Put a necessary comparison rule once in the analysis, not in every row.
7. **Citation relocation:** when removing a defensive clause, keep citations attached to the factual claim they support. Do not drop a useful citation along with the deleted wording.

## Preserve truth-changing qualifications

Keep qualifications that change the claim's truth, scope or interpretation: dataset and protocol boundaries, task setting, assumptions, label budget, uncertainty, confidence interval, sample/population constraints, conflicting findings, causal limits, and conditions under which a mechanism applies. Also retain a concise, specific limitation when omitting it would mislead the reader. A useful test is: would deleting this phrase make the claim materially less accurate or more general than its evidence? If yes, keep or sharpen it.

If access to a source is limited, do not replace an access disclaimer with a stronger unsupported claim. Seek an accessible source, state only what the inspected source establishes, or omit the detail. Store the access limitation in the evidence ledger; include it in manuscript prose only when it materially changes interpretation and the review's methods or limitations section is the right place.

## Acceptance rule

The primary agent scans the full artifact paragraph by paragraph, including captions and tables. If any removable defensive wording remains, reject the chapter and delegate a revision; do not edit the prose as the primary agent. The acceptance report must include a passing defensive_writing check and specific evidence that the scan covered the submitted artifact. Repeat this check after every revision and in the final manuscript audit.
